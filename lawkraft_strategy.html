A real business, in a genuine gap

Lawkraft is a real business making real money, sitting in a genuine gap in the market. Post-judgment recovery, the 90-plus-days-past-due end of debt collection, is still run on spreadsheets and local lawyers at most lenders. You're not fighting for space in a crowded field. You're one of the only organized players in an empty one.

Reporting is ready to send today, nothing to build specially. Once a client signs, the work runs itself, no fresh approval needed batch to batch. Whatever's holding growth back, it isn't delivery.

02 · What You're Missing

Five gaps, none of them about delivery

  • A written ICP. Right now, who's worth chasing lives in your head, not on a page the team can actually use. Fine at 3 people. Won't hold as you grow.
  • One consistent answer to "why us." Ask three people on your team why a bank should pick Lawkraft over the law firm they've used for ten years, and you'll get three different answers. That's a tax on a sales cycle that's already running past 3 months.
  • A written compliance answer. If a bank's risk team asked today how you handle debtor data and whether your advocates follow RBI's fair-practice rules, you'd be putting something together on the spot. That's exactly the question the bigger buyers you're chasing will ask first.
  • A pricing stance. When a rival drops price 30% to win an account, your answer today is "depends on the account." That's not a position you can repeat, and it'll get tested more as you move up-market, not less.
  • A process for growing inside clients you already have. Once a client's first batch of 50 cases is running, whether you go after the rest of their portfolio depends on someone remembering to ask. That's revenue sitting on the table with people who already trust you.
03 · The Real Gap

A visibility problem, not a targeting one

Here's the sharpest thing I found in your answers, and I want you to sit with it for a second. You identified ARCs as the right market, unprimed, twice. You've had real conversations with lenders outside your current base. You're bootstrapped and careful with money. And yet right now, someone running collections at a large Asset Reconstruction Company has never heard of Lawkraft.

What it means

This isn't a targeting problem. You already solved that part yourself. It's a visibility problem. You picked the right market, and nobody in it knows you're there yet.

The second gap is smaller, but worth saying plainly: new business still runs through a founder being in the room. Your delivery side could take on more clients without blinking. The constraint isn't capacity, it's that growth still depends on you and your co-founders personally showing up to close it.

04 · Course Correct

Four moves that fix the diagnosis above

  • Stop asking "who else could we sell to." You already answered that one. The real question now is how people running collections at ARCs actually find out you exist. A narrow, targeted push aimed at getting known by that specific buyer, not broad brand marketing.
  • Write the ICP and the "why us" answer down, two one-pagers the whole team actually uses. Fixes the instinct-based targeting and the inconsistent pitch in one move, and it costs you a few hours, not a budget line.
  • Build the compliance one-pager now, before a deal is sitting on it. Turns "we'd need to scramble" into something you hand over the same day it's asked for.
  • Turn "next batch" into a scheduled step inside the six-stage process you already run, not something that depends on memory. Cheaper revenue than any new logo, because the trust's already built.

06 · Discipline

The approach that holds across both the 90-day base and the 180-day stretch

  • Make the ICP and "why us" documents real and written, not something living in your head. Your team can't be consistent with an instinct they were never given.
  • A weekly, founder-led review of the ARC pipeline specifically, kept separate from general business development. The right priority loses out to day-to-day delivery work too easily without its own slot on the calendar. Keep this cadence running the full 180 days, not just the first 90, since the ARC payoff lands in the second half.
  • A standing, calendared check at the "next batch" decision point for every existing client, so account growth stops depending on someone remembering.
  • Decide your price-war stance in advance, not deal by deal. What you'll hold firm on and what you'll flex on, written down once, so "depends on the account" turns into a rule instead of an improvisation.
  • New business still needs a founder in the room, so be deliberate about which room that is. Spend that time on ARC and partnership conversations specifically, the two channels with the most leverage, not spread thin across general outreach.
07 · Second Round

What your drilldown answers told me

Your six answers this round line up with something I already had a hunch about, which is exactly why I kept asking you about ARCs specifically instead of taking the first round at face value. They don't change the diagnosis in section 3. If anything, they confirm it.

Start with the ARC conversations themselves. You called them early exploratory talks, nothing concrete yet. That's exactly what I'd expect from a market that doesn't know you exist, so no surprise there. And when I asked what would actually slow you down once ARC interest starts coming in, you said nothing structural, that you'd just need the conversations to happen. That's the clearest confirmation I've gotten that this really is a visibility problem and not a capability one. You believe you can close it once it shows up. I believe you too.

What it means

But what you told me on WhatsApp about how ARCs actually work changes what "closing it" means. You said ARCs aren't what they look like from outside, that on paper they own the bad loan but the original lender is still the one running the file behind the scenes, and that getting an ARC to the table means working through that multi-party structure at margins that are already thin.

I'm raising this plainly because it's a real constraint, not because it caught me off guard. It doesn't mean stop chasing ARCs, you picked the right market and the visibility push still stands. It means weighing this properly and exactly, the real time, structuring and margin an ARC deal takes, rather than chasing it because it's the bigger, more prestigious name to land. That's a different test than "is this the right market," and it deserves its own honest answer, not a prestige one.

Here are three concrete ways to work around that constraint, not just live with it:

  • Go in through the lender, not the ARC. You told me yourself the original lender keeps running the file behind the scenes. That's not just a complication, it's the door. Get positioned with the bank or NBFC itself, before or right at the point they sell a retail pool to an ARC, so you're already the recovery executor of record when the ARC relationship kicks in, instead of cold-pitching a brand-new counterparty from zero.
  • Chase the pool, not the relationship. Don't try to land "an ARC" as one big logo upfront, that's exactly the multi-party negotiation that eats time and margin before you've proven anything. ARCs regularly buy specific retail and unsecured pools, Bandhan Bank alone sold nearly Rs. 7,000 crore of exactly this kind of portfolio to ARCs in a single deal in November 2025, and a recovery mandate on one pool is a far smaller, contained ask than an enterprise-wide tie-up. Win one pool, then expand from inside it.
  • Price for their margin, not yours. Public numbers on retail NPA sales back up what you told me, Ujjivan Small Finance Bank's microfinance portfolio sold to an ARC recovered just 9.39% of face value, a haircut north of 90%. That's the real economics behind an ARC deal, and your flat per-case fee wasn't built for it. A separate, success-fee-heavy structure for ARC-sourced work specifically, low or no base fee, a real percentage of what actually comes back, moves your economics with theirs instead of against them.

One more thing worth using. You said the deals that stall usually get stuck in the lender's internal approval process, not with you. That's not something you control directly, but it tells you where to put effort: the compliance one-pager from section 4 isn't just useful for your own pitch anymore, it's something your contact inside the lender can hand upward to move their own approval along faster. Build it for them, not just for you.

Why not more, and why not sooner

Housing finance companies, the broader NBFC and nationalised-bank unsecured book, and ARCs are all real, but every one of them runs through a slower, more structured sales or empanelment process, the kind that outlasts a 90-day window even when you start today. Stretching the horizon to 180 days is what lets the ARC push and the nationalised-bank door actually land inside this report's numbers instead of getting waved at as "someday." Even then, the broader NBFC book is honestly more likely a next-quarter story than a 180-day one, I'd rather tell you that now than have it quietly slip and have you wondering why.

Meanwhile, the stable-but-unnurtured biggest client is worth a direct conversation inside the first 90 days, not next quarter. It's not a new logo, so it won't move either client count, but reconnecting there protects revenue that's already concentrated at 20 to 40%, and it's the fastest-moving lever you have right now.

10 · One More Thing

Two questions worth sitting with

Your pricing model. You charge a flat fee per case today, no matter the recovery. But the numbers you lead with, over 70% recovery rate, Rs.1 Cr+ recovered monthly, are outcome numbers. A pure per-case fee doesn't let that advantage show up in the price itself. I'm not telling you to change it. I'm raising a real question worth sitting with: would an outcome-linked or hybrid pricing piece make your real differentiation harder to wave away, especially when you're already having to argue why the Credgenics comparison doesn't hold?

Revenue concentration. Your single biggest client is 20 to 40% of revenue today. Not an alarm on its own, but worth tracking deliberately over the full 180 days as new clients come in, so growth doesn't quietly trade one concentration risk for another.

12 · To Discuss on a Call

Judgment calls, not fixes

A few things here are better worked through out loud than written as a recommendation, because they're judgment calls, not fixes. Noting them so neither of us loses track, for whenever you're ready to go deeper than this report, on a retainer:

  • Whether ARC is still worth the same share of founder time now that you have three concrete ways to work it, or whether even the leaner version of it should rank behind the revenue-share channel for the next two quarters.
  • Whether to formalize "go in through the lender" as a standing part of your sales process generally, not just an ARC-specific move, given how much of the real decision-making already happens there.
  • Whether the outcome-linked, success-fee pricing idea, from section 10 and the ARC-specific version in section 7, is worth testing on a single client first, rather than deciding it in the abstract.
  • Whether housing finance companies and the broader NBFC book deserve a dedicated push next quarter, once cooperative banks and credit societies have proven the volume approach out in the first 90 days.
  • Whether it's worth scoping any engagement between us to the full 180 days rather than just 90, given that the honest numbers above show the real second wave of wins, the first ARC client especially, lands in the back half, not the front.
