An Expert's Guesswork. Would You Bet Your Runway On It?

You only go looking for a mentor when you already know there's a gap, somewhere you can't see your own blind spot, or don't have the pattern-matching to judge a decision properly yourself. That's precisely the moment you're most exposed, and exactly where you're better off under a mentor's wings rather than figuring it out alone. A great coach doesn't hand you answers, they ask the right questions. But confidence and competence sound identical from where you're sitting, and you're the one person in the room least equipped to tell them apart in the exact area you asked for help with in the first place.

The data backs your instinct to go looking for help: startups with genuinely helpful mentors raise 7x more money and grow their user base 3.5x faster, according to the original Startup Genome founder study. What the same study found, and what rarely gets quoted, is that investors' own hands-on involvement showed almost no effect on outcomes. Seniority didn't move the needle. Fit did. Here's how to tell the difference before you've spent six months finding out the hard way, and why you're structurally the wrong person to run that check alone.

What "Helpful" Actually Looks Like

Razorpay's Harshil Mathur and Shashank Kumar once spent weeks courting an investor who kept scheduling more meetings without ever discussing terms. Their mentor at Y Combinator asked one question: had they checked who this person actually was, and whether he had the authority to decide anything? He didn't, he was an analyst. One sharp question, grounded in actually knowing how fundraising works, saved them weeks they'd otherwise have burned. That's the whole difference this issue is about, in miniature. It's also exactly the kind of question the founders couldn't have thought to ask themselves, which is the point.

Global Signal

The Expertise That Didn't Transfer

Quibi launched in April 2020 with $1.75 billion raised across its funding rounds, backed by every major Hollywood studio and several Wall Street banks. It was co-founded and run by Jeffrey Katzenberg, former Disney Studios chairman and DreamWorks co-founder, and Meg Whitman, former CEO of eBay and HP. Between them, they had built or run some of the most successful media and technology businesses of the last three decades. Six months after launch, Quibi shut down.

What the resume didn't cover: the product's entire premise was mobile-native, "quick bite" video built for a phone-first, highly social audience. Quibi blocked users from taking screenshots of its content, to protect it from piracy. That single decision meant viewers couldn't create the reaction GIFs, memes, or social clips that turn a show into a cultural moment. Katzenberg's expertise was in premium scripted television built for appointment viewing and traditional distribution. Whitman's was in enterprise operations at global scale. Neither background included the specific, unglamorous skill the product actually needed: understanding how content spreads on a phone when nobody can clip it.

What it means

Psychologists call this the halo effect: when someone has succeeded at a high level, we assume their judgment transfers to adjacent, unrelated problems. "Being successful in the past doesn't necessarily dictate how successful you'll be in the future," as one retrospective on Quibi put it, and the company's own board, investors, and advisors appear to have made exactly that assumption. Nobody on the inside seems to have asked whether two executives from traditional media and enterprise tech were the right people to judge a mobile-social product decision. The résumés were real. The specific expertise the decision needed wasn't in either of them. If you can't say with certainty whether the expertise sitting on your own team or advisory board actually covers the decision in front of you, that's worth finding out while it's still a conversation, not a $1.75 billion postmortem.

Every major Hollywood studio and several of Wall Street's most sophisticated investors backed Quibi. None of them appear to have asked the one question that would have mattered: who on this team actually understands why people share a 10-second clip? That's not a knock on any one investor. It's the halo effect operating exactly as it's designed to.

India Signal

The One Founder Who Could Trust His Own Read, And Why That's Rare

...and what it actually took him to get there.

Zerodha: Nithin Kamath co-founded Zerodha in 2010 and has never raised a rupee of venture capital. In the fiscal year ended March 2025, the company posted ₹8,847 crore in revenue and ₹4,237 crore in net profit, fully bootstrapped, while remaining India's largest stockbroker with roughly 7.58 million active clients and a 15.58% market share. Kamath has been explicit, repeatedly and publicly, about rejecting the standard VC-growth playbook for his specific business, and the easy version of this story is "he trusted his gut over the experts." That's not quite what happened.

What actually backed his read: Kamath started trading at 17, managing his father's account. For the next 13 years, before he ever founded Zerodha, he worked as a proprietary trader, then as a sub-broker for a Reliance Money franchise, personally providing portfolio advisory services to retail clients. By the time outside voices told him to chase growth the way every other funded startup does, more trades, more loans, more products sold, he'd already spent over a decade seeing exactly what that kind of pressure does to a retail customer sitting across the table from an advisor. "We don't need the money, so there's no point in raising money just because someone is ready to give it to you," he's said. He wasn't guessing that the standard playbook didn't fit. He'd lived the specific mechanism by which it would have hurt his own customers.

What it means

Kamath could overrule the standard advice because he'd already put in thirteen years building the exact expertise that advice was missing. That's not a repeatable move, it's a rare one. Most founders reading this haven't spent over a decade as a trader and client advisor before starting their company, in whatever field they're in now. Without that depth to fall back on, "trusting your gut" and "ignoring good advice because it's inconvenient" look identical from the inside. The honest reading of Kamath's story isn't "trust yourself." It's that the clarity he had took him thirteen years to build, and if you don't have your own version of that yet, you need it to come from somewhere else, specifically, from someone who already has it and has no reason to tell you what you want to hear.

Kamath didn't out-guess the standard advice. He out-qualified it. That's a much harder bar, and most founders are being asked to clear it with far less than thirteen years behind them.

Two Ways The Same Gap Costs You

Neither story is really about one side having expertise and the other lacking it. Katzenberg and Whitman had plenty, just not in the right place. Kamath had exactly the right kind, built over thirteen years before he needed it. Most founders reading this have neither: not Katzenberg's résumé to lean on, and not Kamath's thirteen years in the exact seat they're sitting in now. That's not a gap you close by being more confident in the room. It's a gap you close by having someone else in the room who already has the pattern-matching you don't.

How To Tell The Difference

Four Checks Before You Trust The Advice

A framework worth borrowing, built by GrowthMentor for exactly this problem: telling a mentor who knows from one who's good at sounding like they do.

Check What it actually verifies
Context Check Whether this person solved your exact problem, not just one that sounds similar
Timeline Test Whether they're still close to how things are actually played now, or running on tactics that worked five-plus years ago
Nuance Test Whether they say "it depends" and name real failure modes, or execute confidently either way
Stakes In The Game Whether this person has something real to lose, their credibility with you, a referral, repeat business, if the advice turns out to be wrong for your business

"If someone can't tell you when their advice wouldn't work, they don't understand when it would." — GrowthMentor

You can run this on a prospective mentor cold. It's much harder to run it on someone you've already decided to trust, because by the time you're taking their advice seriously, you're no longer looking for reasons to doubt them. That's exactly the moment an outside, unsentimental read is worth the most, and exactly the moment you're least likely to go get one on your own.

A Note, Not A Case Study

I've sat on both sides of this table. In a past role, part of my job was reading funding proposals for an investment firm and testing whether the growth story behind the ask would survive contact with reality, not whether the deck sounded confident. On the other side, I've spent over two decades in B2B growth and marketing, including mentoring founders directly. I've watched real pattern-matching and confident-sounding guesswork look identical in the room, right up until someone actually tested it. If there's a growth number, a mentor's advice, or a decision sitting in your inbox right now that nobody unaffiliated has pressure-tested yet, that's usually the most useful place to start a conversation, not the deck.

Lessons Learnt

What Quibi And Zerodha Actually Have In Common

A $1.75 billion media collapse and a bootstrapped Indian brokerage look like they have nothing to do with each other. Pulled apart, they're the same lesson about expertise, told from opposite ends.

A great résumé isn't the same thing as relevant pattern-matching. Katzenberg and Whitman had more combined business experience than almost any mentor a founder will ever meet. None of it was in the specific terrain, mobile-native social video, that their own product needed most.
Good mentors say "it depends." Hollow ones say "do this." Confidence is the easiest thing to fake and the first thing founders mistake for competence. The Nuance Test exists precisely because a mentor who's actually solved your problem knows exactly where their advice stops working.
Kamath could trust his own read because he'd already paid for it, in years. Thirteen years of trading and advising clients bought him the standing to overrule the standard VC-growth playbook. That's not a shortcut you can copy by being more confident. It's a depth you either have or you don't, and most founders reading this don't have it yet in the specific area where they're currently taking advice.
The expertise paradox cuts both ways, and it's exactly why you need outside help, not a checklist. You often can't judge, in the moment, whether a mentor's advice actually fits your situation, that's the whole reason you went looking for a mentor. Checks like the Timeline Test and Stakes In The Game help, but running them objectively on someone you already trust is the one step most founders can't do alone, because the trust itself is what gets in the way.

Katzenberg and Whitman had expertise with nowhere to apply it. Kamath had to build his own expertise over thirteen years because he wasn't going to bet his business on someone else's confident guess. Most founders have neither the depth to self-correct nor thirteen years to go build it. What they have instead is the option to borrow someone else's, from a mentor who'll tell them the uncomfortable thing before it costs them, not after.

FAQ

Telling real mentorship from confident guessing

Isn't experienced advice always better than no advice at all?
Not necessarily. Experience in the wrong context can be worse for you than no mentor at all, because it arrives with a confidence that's genuinely hard to challenge. Quibi's leadership had more combined experience than most startups ever get access to, and that experience is a large part of why nobody inside the company pushed back on a product decision that needed a completely different kind of expertise to catch.
What's the actual difference between a good coach's questions and a bad one's?
From where you're sitting, often nothing. Same tone, same open-ended question, same thoughtful pause before they ask it. The difference is upstream of the question itself: a good coach asks what they ask because they've actually lived your specific problem before, and your answer changes what they ask next. A hollow version is performing the same posture without that history behind it, so your answer doesn't really change anything for them. You usually can't tell which one you're getting from a single conversation. That's exactly why it's worth getting a qualified outside read on the mentor you're already leaning on, rather than guessing.
How do I actually run a Context Check in a real conversation?
Ask them to describe the closest situation they've personally handled that matches yours, in specific detail, not in general principles. Vague answers, or answers that stay at the level of "I've seen this a lot," are the tell. Someone who's actually solved your type of problem can usually describe it with uncomfortable specificity. If you're not confident you'd catch a vague answer dressed up as a specific one, that's usually the first thing worth bringing to someone who does this for a living, rather than trying to self-diagnose it.
Is it ever right to ignore a mentor's advice entirely?
Rarely, and even Kamath's case is less "trust yourself" than it looks. He could tell the standard VC-growth playbook didn't fit his business because he'd spent thirteen years trading and advising clients before he ever started the company, a specific, hard-won depth most founders haven't had the years to build yet. Without that depth to fall back on, trusting your gut and ignoring good advice because it's inconvenient look identical from the inside. The safer version of Kamath's move, if you don't have his thirteen years behind you, is getting a second, specifically qualified opinion before you decide the popular advice doesn't apply to you, not deciding that alone.
Does a more senior or more famous mentor mean more reliable advice?
No, and this is the halo effect operating in plain sight. Seniority and fame are evidence someone was good at something, at some point, in some context. They're not evidence that it transfers to your specific, current problem. Quibi had two of the most decorated executives in business running it directly into a mistake neither of their résumés would have predicted.
What's the single fastest way to spot a mentor who's guessing?
Ask them when their advice would be wrong for you. Someone who actually understands your problem can usually name the conditions where their own advice fails. Someone who's pattern-matching from confidence rather than experience tends to answer every version of the question the same way: "it depends" is the sound of real expertise; a flat, universal answer usually isn't. And if you can't get a straight answer to that question from someone you're already paying for advice, that's an answer too.

Think about the last piece of advice you took on faith this year, because you didn't have the specific expertise to check it yourself, and nobody in the room was blunt enough to ask the question that would have tested it. That's not a confidence problem. It's a coverage gap, and it's exactly the gap this issue is about. Would that advice survive the four checks above? If you're not sure, you already know what to do with that uncertainty before it gets expensive.
