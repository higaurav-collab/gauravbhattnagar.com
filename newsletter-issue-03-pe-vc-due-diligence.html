Every Deal Gets a Clean Opinion on Its Numbers, Never on the Growth Story.

Every portfolio company you have ever closed came with an audited balance sheet. Somebody reviewed the receivables, tested the revenue recognition, and signed an opinion that says the numbers are real. Nobody did the same for the growth story sitting next to those numbers: the CAC that justifies the multiple, the channel mix the model assumes stays stable, the retention curve the next three years of returns depend on.

That gap is not a theory. It is the reason audited companies have collapsed after close, and the reason funds are now being asked by their own LPs how they missed it.

If you read nothing else

  • Financial and legal diligence are mature, audited disciplines. Commercial and marketing diligence still runs on CIM slides and a confident call.
  • The industry-standard CAC:LTV benchmark (3:1 to 4:1) is rarely tested against a CAC calculation that actually includes personnel and tech cost, or against attribution that isn't last-click.
  • GoMechanic passed two clean audits before its growth numbers collapsed. The auditors were checking the wrong ledger.
  • India's PE/VC market moved toward more, smaller, more selective deals in 2025, which raises rather than lowers the cost of missing this gap.
  • The fix is a short, specific checklist, not a bigger data room.
Global Signal

The Diligence That Never Got Built

Financial due diligence has a century of audit-firm rigor behind it. Commercial and marketing due diligence is still, in practice, a handful of CIM slides and a management presentation, because the discipline matured later and nobody owns it the way an auditor owns a balance sheet.

What it means

A deal team that is rigorous about EBITDA adjustments and net working capital can still be looking at a CAC number missing a third of its real cost, and an attribution model hiding which channel the growth actually depends on. The financial model clears. The underlying engine does not.

None of this shows up in a financial audit. It shows up nine months after close, in a board update nobody wanted to write.

India Signal

The Fraud Two Audits Didn't Catch

GoMechanic raised more than $55 million from institutional investors on the strength of inflated sales figures and underreported costs. KPMG and PwC both audited the company in prior years and reported no discrepancies. The fraud surfaced when the company collapsed, not when it was reviewed. SIDBI, a government-backed lender and LP to several of the funds involved, is now questioning those funds' own due diligence process directly: the first time that scrutiny has gone this specific.

What it means

Smaller checks and more deals should mean tighter diligence per deal. Whether it does depends on whether that diligence adds a discipline that does not exist yet, since the existing one, the financial audit, already proved it can clear a fabrication two years running.

KPMG and PwC checked GoMechanic's books. Nobody checked whether its growth numbers meant what they said. That is not a financial diligence gap. It is a different discipline entirely.

Why This Is a Diligence Gap, Not a Market Difference

Read both signals together and the shape is the same everywhere a deal closes. The financial numbers get checked by someone trained to check them. The growth numbers next to those financials get checked by whoever happens to be in the room. What changes between a mature market and India is how the gap gets found, not whether it exists.

The Diligence Checklist

Six Checks to Run Before You Sign

Check What it actually verifies
Rebuild CAC from the general ledger, not the CIM Whether personnel, tools, and agency cost are already included, or quietly excluded
Ask for attribution by model, not by channel Whether the reported channel mix survives a shift away from last-click
Test channel concentration at the account level Whether more than 60% of revenue-driving traffic sits on one channel the company does not control
Independently verify the top 20 logos in the CIM's customer list Whether the "customers" are active, paying, and at the stated contract value
Request cohort retention by signup month, not blended Whether blended retention is hiding a newer cohort decaying faster than the average
Check who reviewed the growth numbers, not just the financials Whether anyone with marketing or commercial expertise reviewed the story, or only accountants reviewed the ledger
FAQ

Commercial due diligence, plainly explained

What is commercial or marketing due diligence, and how is it different from financial due diligence?
Financial due diligence verifies that the numbers on the balance sheet and P&L are real and correctly stated, usually by an audit firm. Commercial or marketing due diligence verifies that the growth story behind those numbers, customer acquisition cost, channel mix, retention, attribution, will actually hold up after close. The two are run by different specialists, on different evidence, and a fund can have one without the other.
Does marketing due diligence matter for early-stage VC deals, or only PE buyouts?
It matters more at early stage, not less. A buyout usually has years of audited financials to check against. An early-stage company's growth story is often the only evidence a fund has, which is exactly why CAC and attribution claims deserve more scrutiny there, not an exemption from it.
Can inflated CAC be caught without weeks of data room access?
Most of it can be caught with three requests: the raw ad spend by channel and month, the CRM export of closed-won deals by source, and the headcount cost allocated to growth and marketing. A CAC that only matches the CIM when those three are not cross-checked is a CAC worth re-deriving.
What's a realistic CAC:LTV ratio to expect, and when should a different number be a red flag?
3:1 is the commonly cited minimum and 4:1 or higher is considered strong. A ratio that clears 4:1 only because CAC excludes personnel or tooling cost is not a strong ratio. It is an undercounted one.
How did GoMechanic pass two audits if the numbers were fabricated?
The audits were financial audits, scoped to whether the reported figures were internally consistent and supportable by the documentation the company provided. They were not scoped to independently verify the underlying sales and cost data against external sources, which is a separate, commercial exercise nobody had been engaged to do.
What does good commercial due diligence actually produce, if not just a longer report?
A short, specific list of what in the growth story is verified, what is unverified, and what would have to be true for the investment thesis to hold. The point is a decision tool, not a document.

The checklist above catches most of it. What it cannot do is tell you, in the thirty days before signing, which of your current assumptions are the ones quietly carrying the deal.
