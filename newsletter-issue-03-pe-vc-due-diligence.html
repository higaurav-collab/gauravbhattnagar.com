Evaluating a Funding Proposal? Models Get Audited. But Growth Stories?

Every company you've funded came with a financial model and a cap table somebody on your team pulled apart line by line. Fewer came with anyone testing whether the CAC number, the channel mix, or the growth curve behind the ask was real, or just a confident story.

That gap isn't a theory. A widely cited academic survey of practicing VCs found that 9% use no quantitative deal-evaluation metric at all, and 20% don't even forecast cash flows before investing. The team gets 95% of the attention. The growth story gets whatever's left.

  • Even where commercial/GTM diligence exists as its own specialized practice (growth equity, PE), the specialists who sell it describe the typical standard as rushed and shallow, not solved.
  • A separate NBER study measured actual diligence effort directly (via meeting-hours data) and found it drops specifically in hot markets, exactly when growth stories get least scrutiny.

  • There's no clean industry statistic for how often this exact gap quietly kills a deal, and that absence is itself a clue, not a reassurance.
Global Signal

The Diligence That Isn't There

Commercial due diligence for PE buyouts is a mature, heavily outsourced industry: roughly 36,000 engagements run per year, and mid-market PE firms spend an average of $340,000 per deal on it, mostly through external specialist firms. That's not the world a seed-to-growth VC round lives in.

Builder.ai (the clearest global anchor, collapsed 2025): Builder.ai raised more than $450 million at a peak $1.5 billion valuation, backed by Microsoft, the Qatar Investment Authority, SoftBank's DeepCore, and the IFC, on the claim that its "Natasha" AI engine could build apps automatically, "as easy as ordering pizza." In reality, roughly 700 engineers in India were writing the code by hand behind the interface. The company claimed $220 million in 2024 revenue; a post-collapse audit put the real figure closer to $50 million, an overstatement of roughly 300%. Bloomberg's reporting also surfaced an alleged "round-tripping" arrangement with Indian ad-tech firm VerSe Innovation: roughly $60 million in mutual payments between 2021 and 2024 that both sides booked as revenue (VerSe denies wrongdoing, though it has since restated its own FY23 revenue down by about 24%). None of this surfaced in time to stop the raises. It surfaced when a new CEO opened the books in early 2025, after which a lender seized $37 million from the company's accounts and the business filed for insolvency across three countries.

What it means

Financial modeling and cap-table review are now standard practice on almost every round, regardless of size. Builder.ai had audited financials and marquee institutional investors through multiple rounds, and still ran for years on a product claim, an AI that writes code, that nobody outside the company seems to have tested against the actual engineering team. Commercial diligence isn't standard anywhere in this market. Not because funds don't want it, but because even the specialists who build tools and run practices specifically to do this well say the discipline itself is still immature: GTM datasets need specialist interpretation most deal teams don't have, reports get built under days of time pressure, and, in the words of one commercial-diligence advisory to PE firms, "a vendor who confirms the seller's story and hands you a deck of pipeline charts has increased your risk while making you feel better, which is the worst possible outcome." Worth noting too: even the 73% diligence-failure research above doesn't track "commercial/GTM forecast risk" as its own category, which is closer to the gap itself than a stat about it.

Microsoft, a sovereign wealth fund, and a development-finance institution all backed Builder.ai across multiple rounds. None of them appear to have asked the one question that would have unraveled it: who is actually writing this code? That's not a knock on any one fund. It's the actual state of the discipline.

India Signal

The Fraud Two Audits Didn't Catch

...and the $500K deal where nobody needed to commit fraud at all.

GoMechanic (still the strongest India anchor): GoMechanic raised more than $55 million from institutional investors on inflated sales figures and underreported costs. KPMG and PwC both audited the company in prior years and reported no discrepancies. SIDBI, a government-backed lender and LP to several of the funds involved, is now questioning those funds' own diligence process directly.

Medial (Shark Tank India, smallest end of the scale): Medial, a startup networking app, got a deal on Shark Tank India from OYO founder Ritesh Agarwal. Its investor projected 65–70% subscriber renewal rates and profitability within 12 months, based on the startup's own numbers. The app reached 5 lakh users with claimed "10X" growth. It shut down in 2026, having never found a working monetization channel. No fraud here, nobody fabricated anything. The forecast was just never stress-tested, and it didn't hold.

What it means

GoMechanic shows this gap can hide behind two clean financial audits at $55 million. Medial shows it doesn't even need fraud to matter at $500,000, an honestly optimistic forecast that nobody pressure-tested was enough on its own.

KPMG and PwC checked GoMechanic's books. Nobody checked whether its growth numbers meant what they said. Medial's numbers weren't even fabricated, just never tested. Same gap, two very different ways into it.

Why This Is a Diligence Gap, Not a Market Difference

Read both signals together and the shape is the same everywhere a deal closes. The financial numbers get checked by someone trained to check them. The growth numbers next to those financials get checked by whoever happens to be in the room. What changes between a mature market and India is how the gap gets found, not whether it exists.

Lessons Learnt

What These Three Cases Actually Have in Common

Builder.ai, GoMechanic, and Medial look like unrelated stories, a $450M global AI company, a $55M Indian auto-services startup, and a $500K Shark Tank deal. Pulled apart, they're the same failure at three different scales.

A clean financial audit isn't the same thing as a real growth story. GoMechanic passed two of them. Builder.ai had institutional-grade financial oversight through multiple rounds. Both audits did their job correctly, verifying the numbers were internally consistent, and neither was ever asked to verify the business worked the way it claimed to.
Fraud isn't required for this to be a problem. Medial fabricated nothing. Its 65–70% renewal and 12-month-profitability numbers were an honest, optimistic read of early traction that nobody outside the company pressure-tested before the check was written. The write-down looked the same as GoMechanic's, minus the lawsuits.
Scale doesn't buy protection, it buys a bigger number. The same gap that sank a $500K pre-seed deal sank a $450M round backed by Microsoft and a sovereign wealth fund. Bigger checks get more financial scrutiny. They don't automatically get more commercial scrutiny.
Sometimes the story itself is the fiction, not just the numbers behind it. GoMechanic and Medial both had real products with inflated or untested growth claims sitting on top of them. Builder.ai's core claim, that an AI was writing the software, was reportedly false from day one. The sophistication of what gets misrepresented has further to go than the diligence built to catch it.

None of the funds in any of these three deals were careless by the standard most rounds get held to today. That's the actual problem this newsletter is about.

FAQ

Commercial due diligence, plainly explained

What is commercial/marketing due diligence on a funding proposal, and how is it different from financial diligence?
Financial diligence verifies the numbers in the model are internally consistent and supportable. Commercial diligence verifies that the growth story behind those numbers, CAC, channel mix, retention, attribution, will actually hold up after the round closes. Different specialists, different evidence; a fund can have one without the other.
Does this matter more at seed stage or growth stage?
More at seed, not less. A growth-stage company usually has a couple of years of real data to check against. A seed-stage company's growth story is often the only evidence a fund has, which is exactly why it deserves more scrutiny, not an exemption from it.
Should early-stage forecasts be judged on EBITDA?
No, and this is a common mismatch. EBITDA is a late-stage/PE metric, used once a company is meant to be profitable or close to it. At seed-to-growth VC stage, the metrics that matter are revenue growth quality, CAC/LTV, payback period, and retention, not profitability, because most venture-backed companies aren't profitable yet and aren't supposed to be.
Can inflated CAC be caught without weeks of data room access?
Most of it can be caught with three requests: raw ad spend by channel and month, the CRM export of closed-won deals by source, and the headcount cost allocated to growth and marketing. A CAC that only matches the deck when those three aren't cross-checked is a CAC worth re-deriving.
How did GoMechanic pass two audits if the numbers were fabricated?
The audits were financial audits, scoped to whether the reported figures were internally consistent and supportable by the company's own documentation. They weren't scoped to independently verify the underlying sales and cost data against external sources, a separate, commercial exercise nobody had been engaged to do.
If there's no outright fraud, like Medial, why does this still matter?
Because fraud is the only version of this story that becomes public. A startup that fails after an honestly optimistic, never-tested forecast doesn't make headlines. It just becomes another "ran out of cash" line in a post-mortem. The quiet failures vastly outnumber the fraud cases; we just only hear about the fraud.

Think about the last deal you actually funded. Can you name, right now, who outside that company tried to break its growth forecast before the money moved? If you can't answer in five seconds, you already have your answer, and so will every other fund that's about to find out the hard way.
